# JD Sports Fashion plc: what JD's own documents say about Nike, Jordan and retro basketball (2024 to 2026)

Prepared 2026-10-13. Thesis under test: Jordan Brand revenue fell (FY24 $8,701M to FY26 $7,034M; mid-teens decline in Nike Q1 FY27, Jun to Aug 2026) because Nike deliberately cut retro supply, not because consumer demand collapsed.

- DERIVED: retro basketball share fell about 16.6 pp (from about 36.7% to 20.1%) between FY24 and FY26, a 45% relative fall in mix share, then a further 2.3 pp year over year in HY27.
- JD's spoken gloss, VERIFIED, H1 FY26 transcript p. 8 (24 Sep 2025): "retro basketball went from 20% of our sales in FY20 to almost 40% of our sales two years ago, before slipping back in the last 18 months." OPINION/description.
- VERIFIED, HY27 transcript p. 8 (23 Sep 2026): "This shift of customer trend within our running category is helping to offset the year-on-year mix decline in retro basketball and retro football categories."
- Caveats: (a) "retro basketball" is a category, not a brand. It includes Jordan retros but very likely also Nike Dunk and other brands' basketball-heritage shoes; JD does not split it. (b) This is a share of a footwear total that itself fell (HY27 UK organic footwear c.-6%, Europe c.-2%, HY27 Results Statement p. 6), so the absolute decline is larger than the share decline. (c) JD says its space is not sold to brands ("we don't sell our space... our space is fully flexible", HY27 transcript p. 11, VERIFIED), so the mix reflects JD's buying response to sell-through as well as what brands allocate.

Reading: in Europe the retro-basketball category roughly halved its share of JD's footwear sales over two years while running took the space. JD describes this as a consumer trend shift, not as a supply shortage. Nothing in JD's documents says Jordan is growing in Europe. JD documents mention "Jordan" only as a brand Hibbett carries (H1 FY25 RNS; AR FY25). I found no JD statement about Jordan penetration in Europe (searched all 34 downloaded documents plus a web search).

Launch-calendar shift (a timing explanation the thesis should account for):
- VERIFIED, FY25 prelims RNS p. 8 (May 2025): "North America saw organic sales growth* of 1.4%, reflecting in part a shift in the product launch schedule compared with last year."
- VERIFIED, Q2 FY26 trading statement p. 1 (27 Aug 2025): "in North America we saw an improved performance following the deferral of several product launches from Q1".
- VERIFIED, H1 FY26 RNS p. 16: Community LFL "was skewed towards Q1 (-4.0%) driven by the delay in launches, whilst Q2 saw growth of 1.4%."
- DERIVED: launches moved from Feb to Apr 2025 into May to Jul 2025, which overlaps Nike Q4 FY25 into Nike Q1 FY26. That inflates Nike's Q1 FY26 base, so part of Jordan's mid-teens Q1 FY27 decline is lapping a launch shift. JD does not size it; Community swung from -4.0% to +1.4% LFL between those quarters (a 5.4 pp swing, DERIVED), which bounds the effect only loosely.

Hibbett dependence (VERIFIED via WebFetch of Hibbett 10-K for FY ended 3 Feb 2024, https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1017480/000101748024000043/hibb-20240203.htm; sec.gov blocks curl here but WebFetch opened it):
- "During Fiscal 2024, approximately 74% of our inventory was purchased from one vendor, Nike... who accounted for approximately 73% of our net sales."
- MD&A: "Our largest vendor, Nike, represented 73.7%, 69.9%, and 61.0% of our purchases for Fiscal 2024" (FY2023, FY2022).
- Risk factor: "our sales could decline if we are not provided with a sufficient allocation of high demand merchandise"; "certain merchandise that is in high demand may be allocated by vendors based upon the vendors' internal criteria".
- DERIVED: with Nike about 73% of Hibbett sales, the Community Q2 FY27 LFL of -8.4% is close to a direct read of Nike/Jordan sell-through in that channel in May to Jul 2026.

JD never names Nike as a cause in any of these RNS texts; it says "brand partners", "end-of-cycle product lines" and "product cycle". On calls:
- OPINION, Q2 FY25 call (Aug 2024) p. 5, CEO Schultz asked if Nike newness (Pegasus, Air Force 1) was helping: "No. We are not seeing that." and "Nike will be fine... It's just the time to get that in the market".
- OPINION, H1 FY26 call p. 12, Michael Armstrong: "we are still just managing out of this period where we had three really big items dominating the footwear business"; "getting those newer models and brands up to speed as quickly as the big franchises are declining is still a challenge".
- OPINION, H1 FY26 call p. 18: "the good stuff's really good, the stuff in the middle is slightly more challenging to sell at full price."
- OPINION, HY27 call p. 18 (Sep 2026), Schultz: "There is too much product out there, so customers wait for the discount to come and don't buy immediately, and there is not enough appetite for something new and exciting." and "In the US, it's more around high-heat products and the fact that there are products that everyone wants to buy and is rushing to." and "it's always helpful when the market leader creates it, which is not today the case".
- OPINION, HY27 call p. 16: "our key competitor made the wrong assumption that everything would be rosy by September... they will stop buying for a market that doesn't exist and that means you will not have excess stock."
- HY27 Results Statement p. 16, VERIFIED: Courir (France-led, Europe) revenue fell 9.4% at constant FX "due to a lack of high-heat product and weak consumer confidence in the market"; JD Europe LFL -5.1% with "the leisure footwear market in decline".

## 4. Conclusion: what JD's evidence can and cannot separate

Decomposed by product tier:
1. Limited retro launches (high-heat). Evidence that demand is intact when product exists: "strong demand in new retro basketball product launches" (NA, holiday 2025) and "successful retro launches" (FY26). In May to Jul 2026 JD reports a "slower quarter for high-heat product" in NA and a "lack of high-heat product" at Courir. That wording describes fewer hot launches, not launches that failed to sell. This is consistent with a supply or calendar decision by the brand. JD does not say whether the cause is fewer units, fewer releases, or weaker designs, so the evidence cannot separate a deliberate cut from a thin pipeline.
2. Core and general-release lifestyle lines ("end-of-cycle product lines", "key lifestyle footwear brands"). Soft in every region and every quarter from Q2 FY26 to Q2 FY27, needing "controlled price investments" (group gross margin about -50 bps in FY26, Q4 FY26 update p. 2), with "too much product out there". Excess stock plus discounting is the opposite of scarcity. A supply cut at the top cannot explain this tier.
3. Europe mix. Retro basketball fell from about 37% to about 18% of JD UK/Europe footwear sales (FY24 to HY27, DERIVED) while running rose from about 40% to 58%. JD calls this a "shift of customer trend". No JD evidence that Jordan is growing in Europe.
4. Timing. Part of the Nike Q1 FY27 decline laps a launch schedule moved from JD Q1 FY26 into JD Q2 FY26 (which overlaps Nike Q1 FY26). JD does not quantify it.
5. Third explanation, retailer de-risking: JD stresses buying discipline and says its space is flexible, so lower Nike/Jordan orders may also come from retailers, not only from Nike or end consumers.

Verdict: JD's evidence supports a split, not either pure story. The "supply cut" thesis fits the top tier: retro launches sold well through holiday 2025, and the mid-2026 weakness is described as a shortage of high-heat product. It does not fit the larger general-release lifestyle tier. There the evidence (soft sales in every region, excess inventory, promotions) points to weaker demand at full price. In Europe, the category share decline is large and is framed by JD as a consumer trend shift. North America looks closer to "supply/calendar driven" at the launch tier, plus a cost-of-living hit to the core consumer. JD does not publish Jordan units, prices or sell-through, so none of this can be measured at brand level.
