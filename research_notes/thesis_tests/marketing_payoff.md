# S_MARKETING: Does Nike's higher demand creation spend pay off?

Prepared 2026-10-13. Scope: academic evidence on advertising and sponsorship returns, Nike's own 33-year history of demand creation, peer case studies, and leading indicators. All Nike figures are from Nike 10-Ks, 10-Qs, 8-K exhibits and SEC XBRL unless marked otherwise. Labels used: **[opened]** = I opened the page or API record and read the figure there; **[secondary]** = opened page, but it summarizes someone else's work; **[snippet-only]** = seen only in a search-engine summary, not verified on an opened page.

Nike terms: "demand creation" = brand marketing (advertising, digital marketing, brand events, retail brand presentation) plus sports marketing (athlete, team and league endorsement contracts, complimentary product, sports marketing events). Source: FY2026 10-K and Q1 FY2027 10-Q definitions. A large part is contractually fixed: endorsement obligations were about $15.5B at May 31, 2026, with about $1.7B payable within 12 months (FY2026 10-K).

## (A) Plain-English summary

- **Advertising works, but less than most people think.** Across 56 studies the average short-term sales "elasticity" of advertising is 0.12 and the long-term figure 0.24: raising ad spend 10% lifts sales about 1.2% now and about 2.4% over time. A 2021 study of 288 TV-advertised brands found even smaller effects and that more than 80% of brands would earn more by spending a bit less.
- **The effect shows up quickly or not at all.** Field experiments find that if extra advertising does not move sales in the first year, it almost never moves them later; if it does work, the total effect over three years is roughly double the first-year effect.
- **Spending through downturns can pay, under conditions.** Firms that raise their advertising share during economy-wide recessions tend to gain profit and market share, especially in cyclical industries. But the payoff depends on the firm (market share, leverage, category), and Nike's slump is mostly a company-specific product and channel problem, not a macro recession where rivals retreat. adidas and On are spending more, not less.
- **Athletes and events can pay, with big tails both ways.** Signing athletes lifts brand sales (one Nike-specific study: Tiger Woods recovered about 57% of his deal from US golf-ball sales alone) and stock prices tend to rise on endorsement and sponsorship news. Scandals can wipe out billions (Woods' 2009 sponsors lost an estimated $5 to $12B of market value).
- **Nike's own history shows no reliable link between raising the marketing share of revenue and faster growth the next year** (correlation about -0.1, R-squared under 1%, n=31 years). Big jumps in the spending ratio (FY1998, FY2008, FY2022, FY2025) were each followed by weak growth, mostly because revenue was falling (which mechanically raises the ratio) and because the downturns had causes marketing could not fix.
- **Peers say product beats spend.** Under Armour kept marketing near 10 to 11% of revenue for a decade and still shrank. adidas recovered (operating margin 1.3% in 2023 to 8.3% in 2025) with only a small rise in marketing share; the hot Originals and terrace product did the heavy lifting. On and HOKA spend heavily and grow fast because their products are in demand.
- **Early signs at Nike are mixed.** Running has grown double digits for five quarters and football kits sold 2.5 times their 2022 World Cup pace before the tournament (company claims). But in Q1 FY2027 total revenue still fell 5% currency-neutral, Nike Digital fell 13%, Greater China fell 26%, and the full year is guided down high single digits. In North America, demand creation rose 16% for revenue growth of 2%.
- **Bottom line:** the spend is a reasonable bet, not a proven one. At textbook elasticities, the extra roughly $470M a year (FY2026 versus FY2024) roughly pays back on gross profit only under the long-term elasticity (0.24) and only if the brand effect sticks. Watch Performance growth versus Sportswear decline, full-price sell-through, North America wholesale, and whether revenue turns positive by FY2028. A demand creation ratio above 11% with revenue still falling through FY2028 would say the money is being burned.

**What the literature implies for Nike.** (1) Expect modest elasticities for a mature, very well-known brand (0.1 to 0.25 at best). (2) The first year is diagnostic. (3) Countercyclical spending pays best in economy-wide downturns when rivals cut; adidas and On are increasing spend, so Nike is not buying cheap share of voice. (4) Spending only creates value if it rebuilds brand equity and pricing power; spend itself has a tiny firm-value elasticity. (5) Athletes and events can create real sales and price premiums, with fat-tailed reputation risk.

**Back-of-envelope using these elasticities (my calculation).** Demand creation rose from $4,285M (FY2024) to $4,754M (FY2026), +10.9% or +$469M a year. At a long-term elasticity of 0.24, sales would eventually be about 2.6% higher, about $1.2B on FY2026 revenue of $46.4B; at the FY2026 gross margin of 42.9% that is about $520M of gross profit, roughly equal to the extra cost. At the short-term 0.12, only about $260M. Measured against the FY2024 ratio (8.3% of revenue), FY2026 spending was about $880M above "old ratio" levels, which would need about $2.1B of extra revenue at a 42.9% gross margin to break even. Conclusion: the spend pays back only if it works at the strong end of the evidence and the effect persists.

**How to read it.** Raising demand creation as a share of revenue has had essentially zero predictive power for Nike's next-year or two-year growth (R-squared under 1%, slope slightly negative, nowhere near statistical significance). Growth in marketing dollars does correlate with next-year growth (r = 0.33), but that is entirely explained by momentum: Nike spends more when it is already growing, and growth tends to persist. Once current growth is controlled for, the marketing coefficient goes to zero.

**Why correlation is not causation here (and why even a zero does not prove marketing is useless).**
1. Reverse causality: Nike sets budgets off expected revenue, so spend follows sales plans.
2. Denominator effect: the share rises mechanically when revenue falls (FY1998, FY2025), and falling-revenue years are followed by more weakness or by mean reversion, both unrelated to marketing.
3. Event calendar: Olympics and World Cups force spending regardless of demand and straddle Nike's May fiscal year end.
4. Omitted variables: product cycles (Shox, Flyknit, the Dunk boom), channel strategy, China, FX, COVID, and inventory cycles dominate.
5. Small sample (31 points), overlapping two-year windows, and a basis break in FY2010/FY2011.
6. Counterfactual is unobserved: revenue might have fallen more without the spend. Annual company data cannot answer that; only experiments or market-level variation can.

**adidas under Bjorn Gulden (CEO from January 2023).** Marketing intensity barely moved: it was cut to 11.8% in 2023 (the 2023 report says adidas limited point-of-sale spend when promotions were heavy but kept investing in brand campaigns around major sporting events) and rose to 12.4% by 2025. Revenue returned to growth in year two (adidas brand currency-neutral +13% in both 2024 and 2025 excluding Yeezy, per the opened 2025 management report) and operating margin went from 1.3% to 8.3% in two years. Drivers were product (Samba, Gazelle, Originals and terrace styles, Evo SL running), wholesale relationships and Yeezy liquidation. Marketing amplified a product trend; it did not create one. In Q2 2026 (World Cup quarter) marketing and point-of-sale spend rose 30% (+EUR 212M) and currency-neutral revenue rose 14%, but operating profit rose only 5% [opened: worldfootwear.com]. Even for the brand with momentum, event spend lifted sales more than profit. Time to work: about 12 months to margin improvement and about 18 to 24 months to clear growth.

**Under Armour.** The clearest "spend is not enough" case: marketing stayed around 10 to 12% of revenue for a decade while revenue stalled near $5B and margins collapsed. In FY2025 marketing dollars fell 3.3% "due to a reduction in marketing activities" but the ratio rose to 10.6% as revenue fell 9% [snippet-only for the 10-K wording; the dollars and ratio match XBRL]. UA's problems (product, discount channel reliance, brand positioning) were not fixed by marketing intensity in either direction.

**On and HOKA.** Both spend heavily relative to size and grow fast, but the causality runs mainly from distinctive product (CloudTec, maximal cushioning) and new distribution; marketing raised awareness of products runners already wanted. They are also the share-takers in Nike's running business, so Nike's running recovery must win against rivals that are increasing spend.

**lululemon.** Advertising intensity rose from 4.1% to 5.6% while growth slowed from 30% to 5% and margins fell: more spend did not stop a slowdown driven by Americas product fatigue and competition.

**Nike's 2017 to 2020 digital shift and its aftermath.** Under the Consumer Direct Offense, demand creation share drifted down while NIKE Direct rose from about 28% to 39% of NIKE Brand revenue (FY2017 to FY2021, 10-Ks). It worked for several years (growth plus margin), helped by COVID-era digital demand. Former Nike marketing executive Massimo Giunco argued in 2024 that the 2020 reorganization moved Nike from brand building to digital, data-driven sales activation, increasing reliance on discounting and eroding margins and brand equity; gross margin fell from 46% (FY2022) to 43.5% (FY2023) [secondary: fashionunited.com summary of his LinkedIn essay; original not opened]. This fits Binet and Field's warning about over-investing in activation. The current plan reverses it: Nike is "Repositioning NIKE Brand Digital as a full-price platform and reinvesting in wholesale distribution" and investing in brand and sports marketing (Q1 FY2027 10-Q). NIKE Brand Digital fell 13% in Q1 FY2027 as promotions were pulled back.

**Lessons.** (1) Marketing multiplies product heat; it rarely creates it. (2) Turnarounds that worked (adidas 2023 to 2025, Nike after FY2009) took 18 to 36 months. (3) Event spend lifts sales and visibility in-quarter, but incremental margins are thin even for winners. (4) Shifting brand money into short-term digital activation can look efficient for years and then show up as brand erosion and discount dependence.

## (F) Verdict

**What it would take for the spend to pay off.**
- Revenue must return to growth and the demand creation ratio must fall back toward about 9 to 10% through growth, not cuts. On the elasticity math in (B), the roughly $470M a year increase since FY2024 needs about $1.1B to $1.2B of lasting extra revenue to pay back on gross profit, and the full step-up above the old 8.3% ratio (about $880M) needs about $2.1B.
- Performance (Running, Football, Training, Basketball) must keep growing fast enough to offset Sportswear and Jordan declines, which management says extend beyond FY2027.
- Full-price realization and footwear ASP must improve while units stabilize: proof that marketing is rebuilding pricing power, the thing that actually drives firm value (Edeling and Fischer; Chung et al. price premium).
- Wholesale partners must sell through without extra markdowns, and Greater China must stop falling.

**Likely timing.** The academic evidence says an effective ad increase shows up within about a year and builds to about twice that over three years; Nike's own past recoveries (FY1999 to FY2002, FY2009 to FY2011) took 2 to 4 years; adidas took about 18 to 24 months. Nike's step-up began in FY2025 (June 2024). With FY2027 guided down high single digits, the realistic window for proof is H2 FY2027 (December 2026 to May 2027) for stabilization and FY2028 (June 2027 to May 2028) for growth. If revenue is not growing by FY2028, the first-year diagnostic from Lodish et al. and Nike's own results both point to a weak payoff.

**What would show it is failing.**
- Demand creation stays at or above 11% of revenue while revenue keeps falling into FY2028.
- Football and Running growth fades after the World Cup quarter (Q2 and Q3 FY2027 commentary), or HOKA and On keep accelerating.
- North America, the region with the biggest DC increase (+16%), stays at low single digit growth.
- ASP and discount drag persist in the gross margin bridge; inventory grows faster than sales.
- Nike abruptly cuts demand creation (as in FY1999 and 2H FY2009) to protect EPS: that would mean management itself no longer sees a return.
- Endorsement commitments keep rising while revenue falls, increasing fixed costs.

**Overall.** The history and peer evidence say marketing is a multiplier on product and marketplace health, not a substitute. The extra spend is defensible as brand repair after the 2020 to 2023 swing toward activation, and the early Performance, Running and Football signals are real but company-reported and concentrated. At realistic elasticities it is close to breakeven, not a large value creator, unless it restores pricing power. Treat it as a cost of the turnaround whose payoff depends on product and channel execution, with a verdict available around mid-2027 to mid-2028.
