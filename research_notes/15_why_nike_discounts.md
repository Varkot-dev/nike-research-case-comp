> Repository copy: CSVs are in `data/discount/`. Key bridge lines were spot-checked against the filing text (Q3 FY26 10-Q: ASP +80 bps; Q2 FY26 10-Q: ASP -50 bps). The three FY2023 10-Qs used here were fetched from the investors.nike.com mirror.

**What "discounting" means in Nike's numbers.** Discounting reaches the income statement through four channels. This note measures each one:
1. NIKE Direct markdowns (nike.com, the app, factory and in-line stores). These lower average selling price (ASP).
2. Wholesale "post-invoice sales discounts" and "sales returns" that Nike grants to retailers. These are booked as a reduction of revenue against the sales-related reserve (FY25 10-K p. 62).
3. Inventory obsolescence reserves. These write inventory down to what it will sell for and are booked in cost of sales (FY25 10-K p. 64).
4. Off-price sales (selling surplus to off-price retailers). Nike reported this as its own line until FY2024.

Since FY2025, Nike's gross margin bridge rolls (1) and part of (2) into one "NIKE Brand ASP" line, together with channel mix and price increases.

## (A) Plain-English summary

- **Two different discount waves, with different causes.** Wave 1 (FY2023) was a glut of goods: ships that had been stuck in 2021 and 2022 arrived early and at once, and inventory jumped 44% while sales rose about 4% (Q1 FY23 release p. 3; Q1 FY23 10-Q p. 21). Wave 2 (FY2025 to Q1 FY2026) was a deliberate clean-up. Nike chose to pull too-common sneaker styles (Dunk, Air Force 1, Air Jordan 1) out of stores by marking them down and by paying retailers to discount or return them (FY25 10-K p. 29 to 30).
- **The size of Wave 2.** "Gross margin" is the share of each sales dollar left after paying for the product. A basis point (bp) is 0.01 percentage point. In FY2025, lower selling prices cut gross margin by about 180 bps, and higher write-downs and other costs cut it by about 90 bps (FY25 10-K p. 35). Together with Converse that is about $1.3 billion of lost gross profit (my arithmetic).
- **The retailer side of the clean-up shows up in a reserve.** Nike holds a balance for the discounts and returns it expects to give retailers. It went from $994M (May 2023) to $1,282M (May 2024) to $1,834M (May 2025), then fell to $1,589M (May 2026) and $1,488M (Aug 2026) (FY23 10-K p. 67; FY24 10-K p. 69; FY25 10-K p. 68; FY26 10-K p. 67; Q1 FY27 10-Q p. 7). Measured against a quarter's wholesale sales, it peaked at 28% and is still 21%, above the 14% to 18% of FY2023 to FY2024 (my arithmetic).
- **Nike's own warehouses are no longer overstuffed.** Inventory has been flat to down year over year for nine straight quarters, and since Q1 FY2026 it has moved in line with sales; Nike says units fell in Q1 to Q3 FY2026 (releases p. 1). Inventory now covers about 100 to 112 days of cost of sales, inside the FY2024 range (my arithmetic). Today's discounting is therefore not explained by Nike holding too much stock overall. Two regions are exceptions: EMEA (inventory +10% vs sales +3% in FY2026) and APLA.
- **Since Q2 FY2026 the margin squeeze has mostly been tariffs, not discounts.** Lower selling prices (discounts plus the shift toward wholesale) took 250 bps off margin in Q1 FY26, then 50 bps (Q2), then added 80 bps (Q3), and only 30 bps in Q1 FY27. Import tariffs took 100, 360 and 270 bps in Q1 to Q3 FY26 (10-Qs). Excluding the $986M tariff refund booked in Q4 FY2026, FY2026 gross margin was about 40.8%, against 42.7% in FY2025 (my arithmetic).
- **Discounting is still visible to shoppers, but it is spread across the range, not concentrated in the old hit styles.** On nike.com on 2026-10-13, 44% of shoe colourways were below full price. Air Jordan 1 (28%), Air Force 1 (37%) and Dunk (46%) were at or below that average. Basketball signature shoes (64%), other Jordan styles (55%) and Air Max (50%) were above it (repo data, my arithmetic).
- **Where Nike discounts more than rivals: specialty running stores.** At Running Warehouse, 49% of Nike road-running colourways were on sale, against 9.5% for HOKA, 12.5% for On and 22% for adidas. At Foot Locker, Nike (13.2% of styles on sale) was in line with the store (11.4%) and below HOKA (16.0%) and On (15.7%) (repo data). Many marked-down Nike running shoes are current models (Vomero 18, Pegasus Premium), not old stock.
- **What the filings cannot tell us.** Nike reports price cuts, channel mix and price increases as one number, and it does not say how much of the clean-up was its own choice versus shoppers paying full price less often. Its newest 10-Q says more clean-up is needed in Sportswear and Jordan "in part" because of "higher levels of discounting and broader marketplace pressure" (Q1 FY27 10-Q p. 20). That is a sell-through signal, but the size of it is not disclosed. Section F lists the data that would settle it.

## (C) Gross margin bridge, FY2023 to Q1 FY2027, Nike's own disclosures (bps)

How to read it. Each number is Nike's stated contribution to the year-over-year change in consolidated gross margin. The "discount-linked" column is my grouping of the price and markdown lines: the ASP line when negative, the NIKE Direct margin, off-price, Converse, and other costs only in periods where Nike names obsolescence. Dollars are bps multiplied by that period's revenue (my arithmetic).

Definitions changed in FY2025. Through FY2024 the ASP line is "full-price ASP, net of discounts, on a wholesale equivalent basis," and NIKE Direct markdowns sit in the separate "NIKE Direct margin" line. From FY2025 one "NIKE Brand ASP" line holds discounts, channel mix and price increases together.

Q4 rows are **implied** by my arithmetic (full year times revenue, minus nine months times revenue, divided by Q4 revenue). Each annual line is rounded to 10 bps, so Q4 values carry roughly +/-40 bps of error. Q4 FY25's implied lines sum to -571 against a reported -440, so read that row as direction only. Q4 FY26's implied lines reconcile to +891 against a reported +890, assuming the FY26 ASP effect was zero (Nike does not list it as a FY26 line). FY2023 has no bps in the 10-K text (the bridge is a chart, FY23 10-K p. 34), so Q4 FY23 cannot be derived. A "." means Nike did not list the line.

What the bridge says, in order:
1. **FY2023: discounting came from excess inventory, and price increases hid it.** NIKE Direct promotions cost 90 to 160 bps a quarter, and product and freight costs cost 190 to 430 bps. Meanwhile "strategic pricing" lifted full-price ASP by 200 to 410 bps. The Q2 FY23 10-Q (p. 24) ties the promotions to "high levels of promotional activity to sell excess inventory."
2. **FY2024: little discounting at full price.** Full-price ASP +200 bps (FY). The early signs were the NIKE Direct line turning negative (-40 FY, about -100 implied Q4) and off-price -20 to -40 bps.
3. **FY2025 to Q1 FY2026: the clean-up.** Discount-linked lines total -290 bps in FY25 (-$1,343M) and -280 in Q1 FY26 (-$328M). Lower product costs (+80 FY25) hid part of it.
4. **Q2 FY2026 to Q1 FY2027: tariffs replace discounts as the main drag.** ASP -50, +80, about +260 (implied), then -30. Product cost (tariffs) -360 and -270. Excluding the IEEPA refund, FY26 gross margin was 40.8% (my arithmetic: $19,911M less $986M, divided by $46,398M; FY26 10-K p. 55; FY26 10-K p. 30) against 42.7% in FY25. That -190 bps sits almost entirely in product cost (about -230 bps excluding IEEPA).
5. Costs below gross margin are not the subject here. For context, demand creation rose from 8.3% of revenue (FY24) to 10.1% (FY25) and 10.2% (FY26) (my arithmetic on FY26 10-K p. 34; FY25 10-K p. 35).

Findings:
- **FY2023 is the only true company-wide glut.** Inventory grew 40 pts faster than sales in Q1 FY23, and Nike said the cause was transit timing, not orders: "inventory transit times improved ahead of plan, particularly in North America ... caused inventories in North America to grow to $4.7 billion" (Q1 FY23 10-Q p. 21). Nike then cut purchases: "we took action to reduce excess inventory by decreasing inventory purchases and increasing promotional activity" (Q3 FY23 10-Q p. 24). By FY24, inventory was down 11% "primarily due to a decrease in units" (FY24 10-K p. 30).
- **FY2025's build was relative.** Revenue fell about 10% while inventory dollars stayed flat, so DIO rose about 7 days at year end (96 to 103). Units rose in Q2 and Q3 FY25. This is the stock the clean-up cleared, together with the returns Nike took back from retailers. The estimated cost of returned inventory rose from $331M to $528M (FY25 10-K p. 83).
- **From Q1 FY26, Nike's own inventory does not explain the discounting.** The gap between inventory and revenue growth stayed within about +/-4 pts, and DIO sat at or below FY24's range (96 to 112, against 98 to 119 for FY24 to FY25). The Q1 FY27 DIO of 112 is 2 days above Q1 FY26, and Nike attributes the change to mix, not units.
- **Regional exceptions:** EMEA (FY26 DIO 112; 121 at Feb 2026), which matches its "higher discounts" margin wording, and APLA (inventory up two years running against flat to falling sales). Greater China's Nike-owned inventory fell faster than its sales (DIO 91 in FY26). The China inventory problem Nike describes is "across the marketplace," meaning partner stores, which are not on Nike's balance sheet (FY26 10-K p. 30).
- **Units vs value:** Nike gives the direction of units only (no unit counts). Units were down in Q1 FY24 to Q4 FY24 and Q1 to Q3 FY26. Units rose in Q2 FY25, Q3 FY25 and Q4 FY26 (releases p. 1).
- **Inventory reserve** (lower of cost and net realizable value): $155M (May 2024), $233M (May 2025), $213M (May 2026) (FY25 10-K p. 64; FY26 10-K p. 63). It is small next to the $1.5B to $1.8B sales-related reserve. Most of the clean-up cost ran through revenue (discounts and returns) and the ASP line, not through write-downs.

## (E) Ruled-out explanations and why

1. **"Nike is discounting because its own warehouses are overfull" (Q1 FY26 to Q1 FY27): ruled out at company level.** Nike's inventory fell or stayed flat while revenue was flat, units fell in four of five quarters, and DIO sat inside the FY24 range (Section D). The sales-related reserve has fallen four quarters in a row ($1,788M to $1,488M). It survives only regionally: EMEA in FY26 and APLA.
2. **"Today's discounting is the old classics oversupply": ruled out for the current nike.com assortment, not for FY2025.** On 2026-10-13, AJ1 (28.0%), AF1 (37.2%) and Dunk (46.2%) colourways were discounted at or below the site average (43.8%). Together they were 7.3% of discounted colourways against 9.2% of the assortment. Numbered Jordan retros were 10.0% discounted. New-with-tags resale asks for Dunk, AF1 and AJ1 Low sit at 0.90 to 1.04 of retail (Poshmark, repo data). The most discounted groups are basketball signature (64.1%), other Jordan (55.1%), Air Max (49.6%) and other performance (48.0%). Caveats: this is a single date. It counts listed colourways, not units sold. It cannot see wholesale partners' Jordan retro stock, which Nike and DICK'S both flag (Q1 FY27 10-Q p. 20; repo note).
3. **"Tariffs are forcing the discounts": ruled out as a direct cause.** Tariffs appear in product cost (-100, -360, -270 bps in Q1 to Q3 FY26). In the same quarters ASP improved, from -250 to -50 to +80, with "strategic pricing" named as a positive (Q3 FY26 10-Q p. 30). Tariffs explain why gross margin stayed low after the discount drag faded. They do not explain discounts. Whether the fall 2025 price increases slowed full-price sell-through remains open (Section F).
4. **"Currency": ruled out** as a discount driver. FX is its own line, and it turned positive in FY26 (+20) and Q1 FY27 (+40).
5. **"Greater China is dragging Nike's selling prices": ruled out at Nike's level.** Greater China's segment ASP rose in FY25 ("strategic pricing actions, partially offset by higher discounts," FY25 10-K p. 40). Its FY26 margin decline was FX (-30 bps). In Q1 FY27 it cited "lower discounts" (Q1 FY27 10-Q p. 28). The China discounting happens in partner stores. For Nike it shows up as lower sell-in (wholesale -14% in FY26) and lost revenue (-26% currency-neutral in Q1 FY27), not as a Nike ASP line.
6. **"Competitors are forcing Nike to match prices everywhere": ruled out outside specialty running.** At Foot Locker, Nike (13.2% of styles on sale) discounts less often than HOKA (16.0%), On (15.7%) and New Balance (17.7%), with similar average depth (25.0% vs 27.5%, 22.3% and 37.5%). On nike.com, running is less discounted than the average. The pressure is real only in specialty running (49% vs 9.5% for HOKA). There, it includes new models (Vomero 18: 17 of 29 colourways on sale; Pegasus Premium: 19 of 27; Running Warehouse items, repo data).
7. **"Supply-chain and freight costs" (the FY2023 story): ruled out for FY2025 onward.** Warehousing and logistics were positive in every period from Q1 FY25 (+20 to +90 bps) except the implied Q4 FY25 and Q4 FY26 values, which are within rounding error.
8. **"Off-price liquidation is the big lever": not testable after FY24.** The line was -20 to -40 bps in FY23 to FY24, and Nike stopped reporting it. Factory-store discounts are named once (Q1 FY26 10-Q p. 19) but not sized. Factory store counts did not grow (US 213 to 212, non-US 560 to 537), so the factory-store channel did not expand in a way that would add discounting.

## (F) What the data cannot separate, and what would decide it

1. **Discount vs channel mix vs price increase inside the ASP line (FY2025 onward).** Nike reports one number ("higher discounts and channel mix, partially offset by strategic pricing"). The rising wholesale share (56.3% to 60.8% of NIKE Brand channel revenue, my arithmetic) lowers ASP mechanically, because a pair sold to a retailer books less revenue than the same pair sold on nike.com. That effect is offset in warehousing (+20 to +90 bps). *Would decide:* a split of the ASP line, or ASP by channel. Second best: the full-price share of NIKE Direct sales. A management remark puts digital at about "50/50" full-price to promotional (Q2 FY25 call, snippet only, not verified).
2. **Deliberate supply cuts and clean-up vs lower full-price sell-through.** The FY2025 filings frame discounting as Nike's own "actions to reduce supply" (FY25 10-K p. 29). The Q1 FY27 10-Q (p. 20) says further actions reflect "in part" market discounting and pressure. Both mechanisms produce the same signs: falling units, lower ASP and higher returns reserves. *Would decide:* (a) the sales-related reserve rollforward (additions vs amounts used), which would show whether new allowances are still being granted; (b) point-of-sale sell-through by franchise at full price from Circana, against Nike shipments; (c) partner weeks of supply by franchise. The North America evidence leans to sell-in exceeding sell-through in FY26: Nike's NA wholesale grew about +9% to +24% a quarter from Q1 FY26 to Q1 FY27 (reported dollars, `data/nike_channel_split.csv`, my arithmetic), while Foot Locker comps were -4.7% to +0.6% and its inventory was up about 17% (repo note, retailer filings). That still cannot be split by brand.
3. **Did the fall 2025 price increases reduce unit sell-through?** Footwear units went from +2% (Q1 FY26) to -5% (Q1 FY27), and nike.com sale share rose from 38% (Aug 2025) to 43% to 44%. Over the same window classics were still being cut and Greater China fell 26%. *Would decide:* unit sales of repriced styles before and after the increase, against unchanged styles, from Circana or Nike.
4. **Specialty running: share loss vs over-shipment after re-entering distribution.** Nike's colourway share at Running Warehouse is flat (about 10%) while half its styles are on sale. That fits both explanations: shoppers choosing HOKA and On at full price, or Nike shipping more than the channel sells after the Fleet Feet and run-specialty push. *Would decide:* Nike sell-in vs sell-through at run specialty (Fleet Feet or Running Warehouse data), and Circana running share by brand.
5. **Size of Greater China partner inventory.** Nike says marketplace inventory is high but gives no number. *Would decide:* Topsports and Pou Sheng inventory turnover days (HKEX filings; not opened in this session) and Nike's share of those inventories.
6. **Obsolescence after Q3 FY25.** Nike merged obsolescence into "other costs" and stopped naming it at company level after Q3 FY26 (positive, "lower inventory obsolescence reserves"). Q1 FY27 names it only for Greater China (Q1 FY27 10-Q p. 28). *Would decide:* charge amounts by quarter (only the year-end reserve balance is disclosed).

**Bottom line.**
- **FY2023:** discounting was a confirmed inventory-timing glut.
- **FY2025 to Q1 FY2026:** discounting was a confirmed, deliberate clean-up of oversupplied classic franchises, paid for through NIKE Direct markdowns, wholesale discounts and returns (reserve +$552M in FY25), and obsolescence. It cost about 290 bps of gross margin, or about $1.3B, in FY25.
- **Since Q2 FY2026:** the discount drag in Nike's bridge has mostly faded (ASP -50, +80, about +260, -30). Margin pressure is now tariffs. Today's visible discounting is concentrated in four places: partner inventories (Greater China; Foot Locker legacy silhouettes), EMEA and APLA, specialty running, and broad nike.com sale assortments outside the three classics.
- **Remaining question:** the filings cannot say how much of that is weak full-price sell-through and how much is Nike's own supply choices.
